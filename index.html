About

The work I enjoy most usually starts with a question that has not been fully shaped yet. I sit down with the people closest to it, work out what a useful answer would change, and build a practical way to reach that answer again. Sometimes that means an agent workflow; sometimes it is a cleaner metric, a small model, or a better data process. I care less about making the solution sound sophisticated than about making it useful and dependable.

Agent workflows. I have built and deployed four LLM applications, covering the unglamorous parts as well as the visible ones: product choices, data models, retrieval, tools, guardrails, deployment, and the bugs that only appear after launch. In Social Memory Copilot, for example, the system checks whether memory is needed before retrieving anything, keeps session context separate from long-term memory, shows where recalled information came from, and asks for confirmation before a write or delete. I use extra agents or model calls when they improve the result, not simply because they are available.

Analysis and modelling. Before the current wave of LLM work, I spent years using Python and SQL for statistics, machine learning, and reporting. That included an NLP and agent-based modelling thesis, quantitative analysis for an NSF-funded study of information sharing during extreme weather, and two years defining KPIs and tracing disputed figures back to their source. That experience still shapes how I build: every impressive output should have a number, assumption, or piece of evidence behind it.

Business problems and delivery. Across more than six government digitalisation projects, I helped clients clarify what they needed, translated that into requirements and metrics, and worked with technical teams through delivery. I also presented the results to people with very different levels of technical context. I learned to listen for what each side is really worried about and to make sure nobody has to pretend they understand the other side.

Geospatial. This is where I started: two degrees in geographic information science and early work on satellite-based estimation for public planning and environmental assessment. It gave me a habit I still value—before trusting what a system says about itself, look for an independent way to check it.

I am interested in applied AI and data science roles where I can stay close to both the decision and the people making it. The best fit would let me understand a recurring problem, build the first useful version, and keep improving it with the team that depends on it. I work in Mandarin, English, and Cantonese.

How I Work

01 · Frame

Understand the decision before choosing the technology

I begin by asking what decision needs to be made, who will use the answer, and what could go wrong. Once that is clear, the right mix of data, retrieval, tools, and human review is usually much easier to see.

KPI Design Requirements Decision Frameworks
02 · Orchestrate

Make the system easy to inspect and trust

Important choices—when to retrieve, which model should answer, what a tool may change—belong in visible, testable logic. I keep state and permissions carefully scoped, plan for failure, and leave consequential actions with a person.

Planning & Routing Tool Use Memory Human-in-the-Loop
03 · Measure

Test the part that matters

I choose a measure the system can genuinely fail: retrieval quality, routing precision, behaviour on unseen classes, or performance against a baseline. I label synthetic data clearly and keep demo results separate from evidence of real-world use.

Offline Evaluation Baselines Error Analysis
04 · Productise

Leave the team with something reusable

When the same question returns every week, I look for a respectful way to remove the repetition: a template, a script, a scheduled workflow, or a shared metric definition. The goal is not automation for its own sake; it is giving people more time for the judgement only they can provide.

Live MVP · Google ADK · Agent orchestration, memory and hybrid retrieval

Social Memory Copilot

Social Memory Copilot helps someone remember the context around a relationship, not just a contact name. It keeps the current person, conversation goal, and next action in view. When memory would help, the system retrieves relevant preferences, events, and commitments and shows why each one was used. Changes to the database stay pending until the user confirms them. I kept the architecture to one main agent with clear, testable stages so that the behaviour is easier to understand and debug.

The retrieval stack combines BM25 and pgvector/HNSW, then applies RRF and a lightweight reranker. On a small synthetic evaluation set, Recall@5 rose from 0.682 for either route alone to 0.889 after fusion and reranking. There are 354 passing tests covering user isolation, prompt injection, tool schemas, fallback behaviour, and confirmation-state changes. The live demo uses synthetic contacts and memories, so its results should be read as a technical demonstration rather than production usage.

Model fine-tuning · Confidence-based routing and handoff

Chinese Intent Recognition & Confidence Routing

I fine-tuned a Chinese intent classifier on more than 11,000 dialogues across 50 intents, reaching 87% test accuracy. I also held back ten intent classes to see how the system responds when a request does not fit the categories it already knows.

The confidence score then becomes a practical routing rule. At a 90% precision target, the smaller model handles 94% of known requests and passes uncertain cases to a larger model. During error review, I separate model uncertainty from gaps in the taxonomy or missing context; those lead to different product decisions, even when they look identical in one headline score.

Sustainability Communication & Behavioral Modeling

For my master's thesis, I filtered 78,116 sustainability-related reviews from the Yelp Open Dataset, used LDA to identify themes, and scored sentiment with multilingual BERT. Alt-data method in progress · Free Sentinel-2 imagery

Orbital Capex

Hyperscalers report AI capital expenditure quarterly; Sentinel-2 photographs many construction sites every five days. Orbital Capex explores whether those images can provide an independent, weekly signal by measuring built surfaces and cleared ground at US datacentre sites. The work has already exposed two useful limits: the standard NDBI index misses high-reflectance membrane roofs, and single images cannot reliably separate grey roofing from dry soil, so the pipeline uses persistence over time instead. Parcel boundaries come from county tax records and are checked against reported acreage. The first series moves with the site's public construction timeline, but the 10 m imagery still mixes roofs with roads and parking. The project therefore remains clearly labelled “Building” until a timestamped prediction can be tested against reported capex.
